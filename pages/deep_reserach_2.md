# European Urban Agent-Based Models: Broad Discovery Corpus
- ## Executive summary
  
  This discovery phase identified **28 distinct European urban ABM model families or closely related application families**, spanning residential mobility and housing markets, segregation and school choice, urban expansion and developer behaviour, integrated land-use/transport, mobility, energy transition, flood risk, pedestrian activity, resource use, and urban health. Related papers were clustered where they clearly share a model/codebase—for example **COMPASS** (Amsterdam/Almere school choice), **ENERGY Pro** (Amsterdam), **MATSim Open Berlin**, and **SILO/FABILUT** rather than counting each publication separately. citeturn23search2turn22search0turn17search0turn13search1
  
  The strongest general pattern is that **empirical initialization is much more common than rigorous independent out-of-sample validation**. Several models reproduce observed spatial or aggregate patterns, conduct sensitivity analysis, or use posterior predictive checks, but genuinely separated temporal/spatial validation remains uncommon. Leipzig, Wallonia, COMPASS, Hamburg pedestrian flows, and the updated Open Berlin scenario provide comparatively strong empirical checking, though by different standards. citeturn15search15turn21search1turn19search3turn17search0
  
  Open reproducibility is also uneven. ReMoTe-S, COMPASS, Geneva DPSIR, ENERGY Pro, MATSim Open Berlin, eqasim, and mobiTopp have clearly identifiable public code or scenario repositories; many older land-use and residential models do not. citeturn23search0turn23search1turn23search2turn22search1turn17search3turn17search4turn18search0
  
  For the **next screening phase**, the eight most promising families are provisionally: **ReMoTe-S, COMPASS, Geneva DPSIR, ENERGY Pro, Wallonia HUEM, Leipzig RESMOBcity, Hamburg pedestrian flows, and MATSim Open Berlin**. This is not yet acceptance under our later hard-gate criteria.
- ## Scope and method
  
  The search was deliberately a **discovery phase rather than a systematic-review claim of exhaustiveness**. I prioritized original journal articles and proceedings, institutional publication records, project pages, computational-model repositories, and official code repositories. The corpus is centered on European empirical or spatial ABMs and deliberately retains some weaker or borderline cases where they are useful as methodological anchors. The aim is to create a literature base before developing our own RQs.
  
  A “model family” means that papers sharing the same substantive model, scenario, or codebase are clustered where appropriate. Thus, the 2024 aggregated-data and 2025 household-register versions of the Amsterdam/Almere school-choice model are treated as one **COMPASS family**; the 2019 and 2025 Open Berlin scenario papers are one family; the 2023 ENERGY Pro application and 2024 MethodsX documentation are one family; and the SILO/MATSim integrations are grouped under **FABILUT**. citeturn23search2turn17search2turn17search0turn22search0turn13search1
  
  In the table, **NR** means that the papers or project records inspected did not provide a single meaningful total parameter count. The complexity labels—low, medium, high, very high—are my qualitative synthesis of the number of agent classes, coupled submodels, spatial/data infrastructure, and computational scale; they are **not author-reported scores**.
  
  “Validation” is used conservatively. Calibration fit, sensitivity analysis, expert plausibility assessment, parameter recovery and posterior predictive checks are reported as such rather than being relabeled as independent out-of-sample validation.

  Three gaps are already visible.
  
  First, **true temporal out-of-sample validation is scarce**. ABM papers often call several different operations “validation”: expert review, pattern reproduction, sensitivity analysis, comparison to an observed endpoint, posterior predictive checks, or parameter recovery. COMPASS is unusually sophisticated statistically, but even its posterior predictive checking is not identical to forecasting a withheld later period. citeturn16view1 HUEM provides a particularly clean historical map-comparison design, while the Hamburg pedestrian model uses independently observed pedestrian activity as external evidence. citeturn21search1turn19search3
  
  Second, **code availability is highly age-dependent and uneven**. Several newer models provide genuinely usable repositories: ReMoTe-S is archived through CoMSES, COMPASS has a GitLab project, Geneva publishes code/data, ENERGY Pro supplies NetLogo/R code, Open Berlin is maintained on GitHub, and mobiTopp is an open-source KIT project. citeturn23search0turn23search2turn15search1turn22search1turn17search3turn18search0 By contrast, many scientifically appealing older models—Leipzig, Vienna Region, HI-LIFE—have no clearly discoverable modern public repository in this pass.
  
  Third, **model complexity varies by more than an order of magnitude conceptually and computationally**. ReMoTe-S or a narrow pedestrian model has a relatively bounded behavioural core; FABILUT links two major microsimulation systems; the Paris health model works with millions of mobile agents; and Aberdeen simultaneously models people, households, businesses, industries and neighbourhoods. citeturn23search0turn19search3turn13search1turn15search2turn13search0 This will become important when we apply the semester-feasibility gate.
- ## Provisional top eight for the next screening phase
  
  These are **not eight selected RQs** and not yet eight models that “pass” our criteria. They are the eight families for which a deeper Step-2 reading appears most worthwhile because they combine empirical grounding, a reasonably explicit mechanism, potential for an existing-RQ Anschluss, and/or unusually good reproducibility.
  
  **ReMoTe-S — strongest straightforward replication candidate.** Its main advantage is the combination of a clear substantive question, empirically derived household/dwelling mechanisms, an ODD-style published description, explicit verification/validation exercises and a runnable Python/Mesa model archived through CoMSES. The model is also much smaller conceptually than the integrated land-use/transport systems. Its weakness is that the original empirical base comes from three Swiss housing providers rather than an entire urban housing market, and its validation is not a temporal OoS exercise. citeturn15search3turn23search0 **Next step:** inspect the repository input schema and list the minimum household/dwelling variables needed to reproduce it; then check whether an EU city or housing-provider dataset supplies those variables without bespoke survey work.
  
  **COMPASS Amsterdam/Almere — strongest candidate for rigorous empirical estimation of behavioural rules.** It is particularly relevant to our concern that agent rules should not simply be invented: the later paper estimates behaviour from household-level register data using simulation-based inference and explicitly tests parameter recovery and posterior predictions. It also has a public project repository and a clear published lineage from aggregate to microdata calibration. The central risk is data access: Dutch household register data are much harder to transfer than an open census table. citeturn16view1turn23search2 **Next step:** determine exactly which register variables enter calibration and which outputs can be reconstructed from open or restricted-access Dutch data; this decides whether replication is realistic before considering another city.
  
  **Geneva DPSIR/TRACES — strongest open spatial residential-choice pipeline.** The paper combines a comparatively understandable relocation mechanism with real geography, rents/housing availability, official statistics and a public-transport API, and the authors make model code and related data openly available. This substantially reduces the risk of spending the semester rebuilding software infrastructure from a paper description. Its main limitations are the Swiss rather than EU case and a validation strategy that is useful but weaker than a clean held-out temporal test. citeturn15search1turn23search1 **Next step:** clone/run the original model and inventory which Geneva-specific inputs are generic open-data fields versus bespoke preprocessing; only then test an EU transfer candidate.
  
  **ENERGY Pro Amsterdam — strongest well-documented open household-adoption model.** The original research paper connects household characteristics, dwelling characteristics, prices and social influence to energy-efficiency adoption, while the subsequent MethodsX paper exists specifically to document the model, spatial microsimulation, sensitivity analysis, validation and reuse. Code and synthetic-population scripts are public. citeturn22search0turn22search1 This is unusually attractive from a reproducibility perspective. The question for us is whether an energy-retrofit RQ would still fit the intended Urban-ABM focus strongly enough. **Next step:** run the repository and identify the subset of inputs drawn from the Dutch Housing Survey versus Amsterdam-specific spatial data; Rotterdam, Utrecht or another Dutch city would then be natural data-availability checks rather than immediate RQ choices.
  
  **HUEM Wallonia — strongest developer/land-use candidate.** This family is particularly relevant because the model contains explicit **developer, farmer and planning-authority agents**, while their decision rules are not merely invented: logistic regression and CA components are empirically parameterized. More importantly, the authors calibrate on historical Walloon change and compare the simulated endpoint with observed land use, and they explicitly compare the hybrid ABM against simpler Logit, CA and CA-Logit approaches. That means the paper already addresses something close to our later **“ABM added value”** criterion rather than assuming ABM is automatically superior. citeturn21search0turn21search1 The principal risk is lack of a confirmed public implementation. **Next step:** check whether source code can be recovered from the authors/institutional materials and whether the 1990/2000 land-use and zoning layers can be recreated from currently accessible Belgian/European datasets.
  
  **RESMOBcity Leipzig — strongest classic residential-mobility reference model.** Leipzig gives us a well-established published RQ, empirically derived household types, an explicitly spatial city model and unusually serious attention for its period to testing behavioural assumptions against observations. It therefore provides a good anchor for an RQ that is a genuine replication, temporal update or transfer rather than a newly invented urban-science question. citeturn15search15 The weakness is reproducibility: it is a 2010 model and no current public codebase was confirmed. **Next step:** obtain the full model specification and make a “reimplementation inventory”: agent state variables, rules, parameters, required historical datasets and outputs used in the original validation. If that inventory is short enough, lack of code may be tolerable.
  
  **Hamburg pedestrian-flow model — strongest narrowly scoped spatial candidate.** Unlike many broad urban-system ABMs, its research object is compact: land use/amenities generate pedestrian movement, and simulated flows are compared with real-world pedestrian observations for external validation. citeturn19search3 This narrowness could be a strength for a semester because it leaves time for actual calibration, repeated simulation and sensitivity work rather than building a “whole city.” The current disadvantage is that I have not confirmed a public model repository. **Next step:** determine whether the pedestrian-count dataset, floor-space/amenity data and implementation are available from the Hamburg project; if the code is unavailable, estimate reimplementation effort from the methods section before retaining it.
  
  **MATSim Open Berlin — strongest fully reproducible large-model benchmark, but also the clearest complexity risk.** The 2019 scenario was deliberately designed around open data and transferability, while the maintained repository now offers reduced population samples and explicit calibration against counts, mode shares and trip-distance distributions; the 2025 update adds automated calibration and broader empirical evaluation. citeturn17search2turn17search0turn17search3 This makes replication technically much safer than most older ABMs, but MATSim itself is a large modelling ecosystem, so it could violate our semester-scope criterion despite good reproducibility. **Next step:** run the **1% Berlin scenario** unchanged and measure the software/data/compute footprint before considering any extension. That simple test would quickly tell us whether MATSim belongs on the eventual shortlist.
- ## Gaps, exclusions and handoff to the screening stage
  
  The discovery phase suggests that the literature pool is now broad enough that we **do not need to invent a research question first**. There are already multiple established RQ families with empirically specified agents and mechanisms: household relocation in Leipzig, Vienna, Switzerland and Geneva; school choice and segregation in Amsterdam/Almere; developer/planning interactions in Wallonia; flood-risk adaptation and development in Camden; household retrofit decisions in Amsterdam; and travel behaviour in Berlin, Paris, Hamburg/Karlsruhe and Zurich. citeturn15search15turn15search0turn23search0turn15search1turn23search2turn21search1turn24search0turn22search1turn17search2turn16search0turn17search5
  
  The biggest unresolved issue is **not finding enough papers**, but separating three quite different notions that appear together in the literature: a model can be empirically initialized, empirically calibrated, and empirically validated, and these are not equivalent. The Amsterdam tri-sector housing model, for example, is richly data-initialized but explicitly does not aim to reproduce historical market development; COMPASS performs sophisticated parameter estimation and posterior checks; HUEM reconstructs historical spatial change; Hamburg directly compares modelled and observed pedestrian activity. citeturn22search2turn16view1turn21search1turn19search3 This distinction should be central in the next stage.
  
  A second unresolved issue is **code versus conceptual simplicity**. Some of the conceptually cleanest models—Leipzig and Vienna Region—are old and lack an obvious current repository, while much larger frameworks such as Open Berlin are extremely reproducible. citeturn15search15turn13search6turn17search3 Therefore, “code available” and “semester feasible” should remain separate criteria rather than treating open source as automatically easy.
  
  A third issue is the **data-access trap**. Stockholm and the later COMPASS work are scientifically attractive precisely because they estimate behaviour from high-quality population registers, but those same data may make replication or cross-city transfer difficult. citeturn7search2turn16view1 Models based on open GIS, census, transport, housing-survey or land-cover data may ultimately be less statistically sophisticated but much safer for an undergraduate project.
  
  The appropriate handoff is therefore:
  
  **Discovery corpus → full-paper extraction for the most relevant families → application of our hard gates → only then development of candidate RQs.**
  
  The **first models I would move into that full-paper screening stage are ReMoTe-S, COMPASS, Geneva DPSIR, ENERGY Pro, HUEM, RESMOBcity, Hamburg pedestrian flows and Open Berlin**. Valladolid and Camden should remain immediately behind them as strong reserve candidates: Valladolid has exceptionally explicit coupling of empirical socio-geographic data with household mechanisms, while Camden includes a genuine developer agent, detailed GIS data and publicly linked model materials, but both involve more coupled mechanisms than the simplest top candidates. citeturn14search0turn14search2turn24search0
